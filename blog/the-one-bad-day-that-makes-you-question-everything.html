The One Bad Day That Makes You Question Everything

September 24, 2026

You can be doing incredibly well for six months.

You're exercising.

You're running.

You're meditating.

You're reading books.

You're drinking more water.

You're working on yourself.

You're writing something.

Building something.

Planning a business.

Learning a new skill.

Saving money.

Changing your habits.

Planning trips.

Maybe planning to climb Mount Everest for all I know.

You have approximately seventeen different plans for your future and, somehow, you genuinely believe you're going to make all of them happen.

You're so positive that you're probably starting to annoy people.

Someone tells you your idea is unrealistic.

You think you'll figure it out.

Something doesn't work.

You try something else.

Nobody understands what you're building.

That's fine.

You can see it.

And then...

You have one bad day.

Suddenly, everything is impossible

Maybe something actually happened.

Something you were counting on didn't work.

You got rejected.

You made a mistake.

You looked at your bank account.

You stepped on the scale.

Something you spent hours creating got absolutely no attention.

Or maybe nothing happened at all.

You simply woke up feeling different.

Yesterday you were planning your future.

Today you're wondering whether you have ever made one good decision in your entire life.

Suddenly the business probably won't work.

The book is stupid.

You'll never get in shape.

You'll never have enough money.

Mount Everest can forget about you.

And apparently you're going to spend the rest of your life lying on the sofa staring at the ceiling.

Quite a dramatic development for a Wednesday.

The strange thing is that nothing actually disappeared

This is the part I keep having to remind myself of.

The work is still there.

The months you spent showing up still happened.

The things you learned didn't leave your brain overnight.

The pages you wrote still exist.

The kilometres you ran still count.

The money you saved didn't become less real.

The habits you built didn't disappear because you skipped one.

Yesterday's progress doesn't get deleted because today feels terrible.

But it can certainly feel that way.

We trust ourselves so much more when we feel good

When I'm in a good place, I can look at a huge goal and think:

I have absolutely no idea how I'm going to do this.

And somehow that doesn't bother me.

I'll learn.

I'll ask.

I'll try.

I'll fail.

I'll change something.

I'll figure it out.

But on a bad day, suddenly I need proof.

Proof that it will work.

Proof that I'm not wasting my time.

Proof that I'll succeed.

Proof that all this effort is going somewhere.

And because nobody can give me that proof, my brain concludes that obviously everything is doomed.

Maybe the problem is making permanent conclusions from temporary feelings

I think this is where I've gone wrong many times.

I treated a bad day like new information.

Maybe I was wrong about everything.

Maybe I should stop.

Maybe this isn't working.

Maybe I'm not capable of doing what I thought I could do.

But sometimes nothing has changed except how I feel today.

And feelings are very convincing.

Especially the bad ones.

They walk into the room carrying absolutely no evidence and somehow get promoted to CEO.

So I made myself a rule

I'm allowed to have the bad day.

I'm allowed to be disappointed.

I'm allowed to do less.

I'm allowed to question everything, eat something unnecessarily comforting and dramatically announce that my entire life plan is ridiculous.

But I'm not allowed to make major decisions from there.

I don't quit the project.

I don't delete everything.

I don't decide my future.

I don't suddenly rewrite the story of the last six months.

I wait.

Because I've had enough bad days by now to know something very important.

Most of them end.

And it is amazing how often the life that looked completely hopeless at 9 p.m. looks perfectly workable again the next morning.

Nothing magical happened overnight.

The plan didn't suddenly become better.

The odds didn't change.

I did.

And sometimes that's all that happened in the first place.
